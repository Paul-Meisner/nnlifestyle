If you've been to a chiropractor, you probably got a diversified adjustment. It's what most practitioners are trained in and what most people picture: the practitioner works through several areas, and you hear the sound people either love or dread.

Gonstead is a different system, and the difference isn't the adjustment. It's everything that happens before it.

## The five-step analysis

Clarence Gonstead spent decades developing a protocol built on one idea: find the specific segment causing the problem and adjust only that. Five forms of analysis run before a practitioner touches anything.

**Visualization.** Watching how you stand, walk and move. Postural asymmetry, guarding, how you compensate.

**Instrumentation.** A handheld dual-probe instrument runs alongside the spine reading temperature differences on either side. Asymmetry can indicate inflammation or nerve irritation at a specific level.

**Static palpation.** Feeling the spine at rest — swelling, tenderness, tissue texture, temperature.

**Motion palpation.** Feeling how each segment moves as you bend and turn, to find the one that isn't.

**Full-spine X-ray.** Standing radiographs of the whole spine, taken upright so the images show the spine bearing weight the way it does in life. Used to identify structural issues, rule out contraindications, and see the disc.

Only then does an adjustment happen — and to one segment, delivered with a specific line of drive, often on specialized equipment like a knee-chest table or a cervical chair.

## What it's actually claiming

The underlying premise is that a specific spinal segment can lose its normal position or motion, that this affects the disc and surrounding nerve tissue, and that restoring that segment restores function. Gonstead puts unusual emphasis on the intervertebral disc as the focus.

Whether that model fully explains what's happening is genuinely debated within the profession, and it's worth saying so. What's better supported is narrower: spinal manipulation has reasonable evidence for acute and subacute low back pain, and moderate evidence for some neck pain and cervicogenic headache. Claims that adjustments treat conditions unrelated to the musculoskeletal system are far more contested and the evidence is thin.

That's not a knock on Gonstead specifically. It's the state of the evidence for manual therapy generally, and any practitioner worth seeing will tell you the same.

## What's different in practice

**Fewer adjustments, more analysis.** A first visit is longer. Expect an exam, imaging and a report of findings before any adjusting.

**Specificity over coverage.** Diversified practitioners often adjust multiple areas. Gonstead targets one segment when possible.

**No neck twisting.** Gonstead cervical adjustments are typically delivered in a seated position with a specific contact, rather than the rotary manoeuvre people associate with neck adjustments.

**X-rays are standard, not optional.** Worth knowing going in. If you'd rather not have imaging, say so at the first call — the analysis is built around it.

## Reasonable questions to ask

- What did the analysis show, specifically, and can you show me?
- How many visits before we should expect change, and how will we know?
- What would make you refer me somewhere else?

That last one is the useful one. A practitioner who can name the limits of what they do is a practitioner who has thought about it.

## Is it better?

There's no good head-to-head evidence that Gonstead outperforms diversified adjusting on outcomes. What it offers is a more systematic process and a more specific intervention, which some people prefer and which makes it easier to know what's being treated and why.

If you like knowing the reasoning behind what's being done to you, it's worth looking into. If you want to be in and out in ten minutes, it isn't the fit.
